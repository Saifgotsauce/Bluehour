Glycine has become one of the most talked-about sleep supplements online, usually with the claim that 3 grams before bed will transform your nights. The real story is more interesting and more modest. There's genuine research behind it, but it's small, mostly short-term, and largely carried out by researchers working for a company that makes glycine.

Here's what glycine is, what the studies actually found and how to think about it.

**The short version:** in a few small Japanese studies, people who took 3 g of glycine before bed reported better sleep quality and less fatigue the next day, and one study measured faster sleep onset. Research in rats suggests it works by slightly lowering core body temperature. The studies were tiny and mostly involved researchers from Ajinomoto, a company that makes glycine, so treat them as promising, not proven.

## What is glycine?

Glycine is the smallest amino acid. MedlinePlus classes it as **nonessential**, meaning your body can make it, and also as **conditional**, meaning it's usually not essential except in times of illness and stress.

It's a big part of collagen, the main structural protein in your skin, tendons and connective tissue. Glycine appears at almost every third position along the collagen chain, which makes it about a third of collagen's amino acids. That's why gelatin, which is made from collagen, is rich in it. You also get it from protein-rich foods in general.

## Why do people take glycine for sleep?

Almost all of the sleep research comes from one group of Japanese researchers, several of whom worked for Ajinomoto, a company that makes amino acids, including glycine. The studies all used the same dose: **3 grams taken shortly before bed**.

### The human studies

**Inagawa et al., 2006**

- **Who:** people with complaints about their sleep quality
- **What was tested:** 3 g of glycine or a placebo before bed, in a randomized, double-blind crossover trial
- **What they found:** better morning ratings of fatigue, "liveliness and peppiness" and clear-headedness

**Yamadera et al., 2007**

- **Who:** people who'd had unsatisfactory sleep for a while
- **What was tested:** 3 g before bed, with sleep measured in a lab (polysomnography)
- **What they found:** people fell asleep faster and reached deep (slow-wave) sleep faster, with no change in the overall structure of their sleep. They also rated their sleep better, felt less sleepy in the daytime and did better on a memory task

**Bannai et al., 2012**

- **Who:** 7 healthy men analyzed, all Ajinomoto employees
- **What was tested:** 3 g or a placebo for 3 nights, with time in bed cut by 25%
- **What they found:** less fatigue on the first day and faster reaction times on a vigilance test. Sleepiness trended lower, but not significantly

### How it might work

A 2015 study in rats, published in *Neuropsychopharmacology*, looked at the mechanism. Oral glycine increased blood flow to the skin of the rats' paws, which let their bodies shed heat and lowered core temperature. The rats fell asleep faster and spent more time in non-REM sleep. The effect ran through NMDA receptors in the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the brain's master body clock, and disappeared when that area was removed.

That fits with what's known about sleep in general: your core temperature naturally drops as you fall asleep. But this was an animal study, so it explains a possible mechanism rather than proving anything in people.

## How strong is the evidence?

Promising, but thin. A few honest caveats:

- **The studies are very small.** The most recent human trial analyzed seven people.
- **They're short.** They tested a few nights, not months of nightly use.
- **There's an obvious funding question.** Many of the authors worked for Ajinomoto, which makes glycine, and in one study all of the participants were Ajinomoto employees. That doesn't make the results wrong, but independent replication would carry more weight.
- **Most participants already had sleep complaints**, or had their sleep cut short on purpose. The results may not apply to someone who already sleeps well.
- **It isn't a treatment for insomnia.** None of these studies tested glycine as a treatment for a sleep disorder. If you regularly struggle to sleep, talk to a doctor.

## How much glycine do people take?

The sleep studies all used **3 grams before bed**. A separate safety study by the same research group gave volunteers **9 grams**, three times that dose, at bedtime. It found no serious adverse events and no next-day sleepiness, though some people had mild digestive symptoms.

Glycine is generally well tolerated, but research on how it interacts with medicines is incomplete. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist first if you:

- **Take clozapine.** In a small 1999 trial, people with schizophrenia who took high-dose glycine (30 g a day) alongside clozapine didn't improve, while those on clozapine alone did. The authors suggested glycine may interfere with how well clozapine works.
- **Take medicine for seizures.** WebMD lists carbamazepine, phenobarbital and valproic acid among possible interactions, along with memantine.
- **Are pregnant or breastfeeding.** It isn't known how glycine affects pregnancy.

## FAQ

### How much glycine should I take for sleep?

The human sleep studies used 3 grams taken shortly before bed. No major health authority has set an official dose for sleep. Check with a doctor or pharmacist before starting, especially if you take other medicines.

### When should I take glycine?

In the studies, people took it shortly before bed. In the 2012 study, it was 30 minutes before bed.

### Is it safe to take glycine every night?

It's generally well tolerated in short studies, and a 9-gram dose caused no serious side effects. But the sleep studies only lasted a few nights, so there's little data on long-term nightly use.

### Does glycine make you groggy the next morning?

In the studies, no. The 9-gram safety study found no next-day sleepiness, and the 2007 study reported less daytime sleepiness, not more.

### Is glycine the same as magnesium glycinate?

No. Magnesium glycinate is magnesium bound to glycine. It does contain some glycine, but a typical magnesium dose provides less than the 3 g used in the sleep studies. Here's more on [magnesium glycinate vs citrate](/blog/magnesium-glycinate-vs-citrate).
