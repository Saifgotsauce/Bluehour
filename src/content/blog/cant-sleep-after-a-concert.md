You get home at 2 a.m., exhausted. Your feet hurt and your voice is gone. You get into bed and... nothing. Your heart's still going, your ears are ringing and your brain is replaying the drop.

Being "tired but wired" after a concert, a club night or a festival set is completely normal. Here's why it happens and what actually helps you get to sleep, and get through the next day.

## Why you can't sleep after a show

Several things are working against you at once.

**Your body is still in "event mode".** Excitement, crowds, dancing and loud music push your body into a more alert state. That takes time to wear off, and lying in a dark room doesn't switch it off instantly.

**Late caffeine is still in your system.** That energy drink at 10 p.m. or the cola with your mixer matters more than you think. In a 2013 study in the *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine*, 400 mg of caffeine taken **six hours** before bed cut total sleep by more than an hour, and the people in the study didn't notice the difference. Check the can: many large energy drinks list 150 mg or more.

**Alcohol breaks up the second half of your night.** A 2013 review of all the sleep-lab studies on healthy people found that alcohol helps you fall asleep faster, but disrupts sleep later in the night and cuts down REM sleep at moderate and high doses. So even if you crash quickly, you're more likely to wake at 5 a.m. feeling awful.

**Bright lights and screens.** Stage lights, phone screens and the ride home all tell your brain it's still daytime, which pushes your body clock later.

**Ringing ears.** After loud music, many people notice ringing or buzzing (tinnitus) that's much more obvious in a quiet bedroom.

**Your body is hot.** Dancing in a packed room raises your body temperature. Your body needs to cool down a little to fall asleep easily.

## What to do in the hour before bed

You can't force sleep, but you can make it much easier to arrive.

1. **Wind down on the way home.** Skip the energy drink for the ride. Water's better, and our guide to [staying hydrated at a festival](/blog/how-to-stay-hydrated-at-a-festival) explains why not to overdo that either.
2. **Have a small snack if you're hungry.** Going to bed starving can keep you up, but a huge greasy meal at 3 a.m. can too. Something small is the sweet spot.
3. **Cool down.** A lukewarm shower, a cool bedroom, light covers. Get the sweat off and let your body temperature drop.
4. **Dim everything.** Low lamps instead of the main light. Put your phone on night mode, or better, out of reach. Scrolling the night's videos at 3 a.m. keeps your brain switched on.
5. **Mask the ringing.** If your ears are ringing, a fan, white noise or quiet background sound makes it much less noticeable than total silence.
6. **Do something boring.** A podcast you've heard before, a few pages of a book, a slow stretch. Give your brain something calm to do instead of replaying the night.

## If you're still awake after 20 minutes

Lying in bed getting frustrated makes sleep harder. Sleep specialists commonly advise getting up if you can't sleep after about 20 minutes, doing something quiet in dim light, and going back to bed when you feel sleepy.

And don't check the time. Working out "if I fall asleep now I'll get four hours" is a reliable way to stay awake.

## What about melatonin or sleeping pills?

Talk to a doctor or pharmacist before using sleeping pills, especially if you've been drinking. Many sleep aids and alcohol make each other stronger, which can be dangerous.

Melatonin is a hormone that mainly shifts *when* your body clock runs. It isn't a knockout pill. If you're thinking about it, ask a pharmacist about the dose and whether it suits you.

## The morning after

How you handle the next morning decides whether one late night turns into a wrecked week.

- **Don't sleep until 2 p.m.** A small lie-in is fine, but sleeping far past your usual wake time pushes your body clock later and makes it harder to sleep the next night.
- **Get outside early.** Bright daylight in the morning is one of the strongest signals for resetting your body clock.
- **Keep naps short.** 20 to 30 minutes, ideally before mid-afternoon.
- **Stop caffeine early.** One coffee in the morning is fine. A third cup at 5 p.m. sets up the same problem tonight.

## Don't drive home tired

The trip home can be the riskiest part of the night. According to the CDC's workplace safety institute, being awake for **17 hours** affects you about as much as a blood alcohol level of 0.05%, and being awake for **24 hours** about as much as 0.10%, which is above the US legal driving limit of 0.08%.

If you've been up since morning and the show ended at 1 a.m., you're in that range even if you haven't had a drink. Book a ride, take transit, crash at a friend's place, or sort out a driver who slept in the afternoon.

## When ringing ears are a warning sign

Ringing after a loud show is common and usually fades within a day or so. It's also a sign your ears took more than they wanted. If the ringing lasts more than a couple of days, comes with muffled hearing, or keeps happening, see a doctor or an audiologist.

Our guide to [protecting your hearing at raves, clubs and festivals](/blog/protect-your-hearing-at-raves-and-festivals) covers how loud shows really get and which earplugs keep the music sounding good.

## Sleep still matters

A big night now and then is part of life. But no routine, supplement or hack replaces sleep. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends that adults get **7 or more hours** a night on a regular basis, and "catching up" on the weekend doesn't fully cancel out short sleep during the week.

So plan around it: earplugs at the show, water instead of a late energy drink, a cool dark room, and a steady wake-up time. Your Monday self will thank you.

## FAQ

### Why can't I sleep after a concert even though I'm exhausted?

Excitement, late caffeine, alcohol, bright lights, a raised body temperature and ringing ears can all keep you alert after a show. It usually settles within an hour or two once you've cooled down and dimmed the lights.

### How long does it take to fall asleep after a concert?

It varies. Many people need an hour or more to come down. A slow wind-down routine (cool shower, dim lights, no scrolling) usually shortens it.

### Should I drink alcohol to help me sleep after a night out?

No. Alcohol can help you fall asleep, but it disrupts the second half of the night and reduces REM sleep, so you wake up less rested.

### How do I sleep with ringing ears after a concert?

Use gentle background sound, like a fan or white noise, so the ringing is less noticeable. If it lasts more than a couple of days or your hearing feels muffled, see a doctor.

### Is it bad to sleep all day after a festival?

A short lie-in is fine. Sleeping far past your usual wake time pushes your body clock later and makes the next night harder.
