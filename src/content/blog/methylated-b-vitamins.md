"Methylated" has become a selling point. B-complex labels now shout about methylfolate, methylcobalamin and "active" forms, and social media is full of people saying regular folic acid is useless if you have the MTHFR gene.

Some of that is real chemistry. Some of it is marketing. Here's how to tell the difference, plus the two numbers on any B-complex label that matter more than the word "methyl".

**The short version:** methylated B vitamins are forms your body can use with fewer conversion steps. They're fine to take, but for most people they aren't proven to work better than the regular forms, and the CDC says people with MTHFR variants can process ordinary folic acid. What deserves more of your attention is the dose of **vitamin B6** and **niacin**, because those are the B vitamins with real upper limits.

## What B vitamins do

There are eight B vitamins: B1 (thiamin), B2 (riboflavin), B3 (niacin), B5 (pantothenic acid), B6, B7 (biotin), B9 (folate) and B12. Your body uses them to turn food into energy, make red blood cells and keep your nerves working normally.

Most of them are water-soluble, so your body doesn't store large amounts of most of them. That's often used to claim that extra B vitamins are harmless because "you just pee them out". As you'll see below, that isn't true for all of them.

## What "methylated" actually means

Strictly, only two B vitamins come in "methyl" forms:

- **Methylfolate** (also called L-methylfolate, 5-MTHF or levomefolate) is a form of folate (B9).
- **Methylcobalamin** is a form of vitamin B12.

Labels often lump in other "active" forms that aren't methylated at all, such as **pyridoxal-5-phosphate** (a form of B6), **riboflavin-5-phosphate** (B2) and **benfotiamine** (a fat-soluble form of B1). "Methyl B-complex" usually just means a B-complex that uses some of these forms.

## Methylfolate vs folic acid

Folic acid is the synthetic form of folate added to supplements and fortified foods. Before your body can use it, folic acid has to be converted, step by step, into methylfolate. Methylfolate skips those steps.

Research reviewed by Examine found that methylfolate raises blood folate levels about as well as folic acid, or slightly better, at equivalent doses. One possible advantage is that it doesn't build up as "unmetabolized folic acid" in the blood, though the health effects of that are still unknown.

But folic acid has something methylfolate doesn't: decades of evidence. **Folic acid is the only form of folate shown to help prevent neural tube defects**, serious birth defects of the brain and spine. The CDC recommends that everyone who could become pregnant get 400 mcg of folic acid a day.

## What about the MTHFR gene?

MTHFR is a gene for an enzyme involved in processing folate. Common variants, especially one called C677T, make that enzyme work somewhat less efficiently. Many people find out they have a variant from a home DNA test, and then read online that they can't use folic acid.

The CDC says that isn't true:

- People with an MTHFR variant **can process all types of folate, including folic acid**.
- Getting enough folic acid matters more to your blood folate level than your MTHFR genotype does.
- Common MTHFR variants are **not a reason to avoid folic acid**.

If you prefer methylfolate, that's fine. Just don't let anyone tell you an MTHFR result means folic acid won't work for you, and if you could become pregnant, talk to your doctor before swapping one for the other.

## Methylcobalamin vs cyanocobalamin (B12)

Cyanocobalamin is the cheap, stable form of B12 used in most supplements. Methylcobalamin is one of the two forms your cells actually use.

Despite the marketing, there isn't good evidence that methylcobalamin works better for most people. Your body converts both into its working forms, and a 2015 review concluded that the coenzyme forms, including methylcobalamin, are not likely to be better than cyanocobalamin for preventing or treating B12 deficiency. The small amount of cyanide in cyanocobalamin is tiny and harmless for healthy people.

Either form is a reasonable choice. What matters most for B12 is whether you get enough, especially if you eat little or no animal food, which is where B12 naturally comes from.

## The two numbers to check on any B-complex label

Because B vitamins are water-soluble, B-complex products often contain many times the daily value. For most of them that's not a known problem. Two are different.

### Vitamin B6

Too much vitamin B6 over time can cause **peripheral neuropathy**: tingling, burning or numbness, usually in the hands and feet.

- The NIH's upper limit for adults in the US is **100 mg a day**.
- In 2023, the European Food Safety Authority set a much lower upper limit of **12 mg a day** for adults.
- Australia's Therapeutic Goods Administration says neuropathy can occur at doses **under 50 mg a day**, often in people taking several supplements that each contain some B6. It now requires a warning on products with more than 10 mg a day.
- In June 2026, Health Canada highlighted a possible risk of peripheral neuropathy with natural health products providing **10 mg or more a day**, and said it would update its requirements for these products to include that risk.

B6 hides in a lot of products, including magnesium supplements, multivitamins, energy drinks and "sleep" blends. Add up every source. If you notice tingling, burning or numbness, stop taking anything with B6 and see a doctor.

### Niacin (B3)

Niacin comes in two main forms:

- **Nicotinic acid** can cause a "niacin flush": warm, red, itchy or tingling skin, usually starting 10 to 20 minutes after you take it. The NIH notes that supplements with **30 mg or more** of nicotinic acid can cause flushing. It's uncomfortable but usually harmless and passes within an hour or two.
- **Niacinamide** (nicotinamide) doesn't cause flushing.

The upper limits differ by country:

- **US:** 35 mg a day of niacin from supplements and fortified foods combined. The limit is based on flushing.
- **Europe:** separate limits of **10 mg a day for nicotinic acid** and 900 mg a day for niacinamide.

## FAQ

### Are methylated B vitamins better?

Not for most people, as far as the evidence shows. They're forms your body can use with fewer conversion steps, but studies haven't shown they work better than regular folic acid or cyanocobalamin for the average person.

### Should I take methylfolate if I have the MTHFR gene?

You can, but you don't have to. The CDC says people with MTHFR variants can process all types of folate, including folic acid. If you could become pregnant, folic acid is the only form proven to help prevent neural tube defects.

### Can you take too much vitamin B6?

Yes. Over time, too much B6 can cause nerve damage in the hands and feet. US, European, Australian and Canadian regulators all set or flag different thresholds, from 100 mg a day down to 10 mg a day.

### Why does my B-complex make my skin red?

That's probably a niacin flush from nicotinic acid. It's usually harmless and fades within an hour or two. Taking it with food can help.

### Why does my urine turn bright yellow?

That's riboflavin (vitamin B2), which is naturally bright yellow. It's harmless.
