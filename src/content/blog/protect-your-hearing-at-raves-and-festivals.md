A big night is supposed to be loud. The problem is that your ears keep score. Clubs, raves and festivals regularly run at levels that can damage hearing in less time than a single DJ set, and the damage builds up quietly over years of weekends.

The good news: protecting your hearing doesn't mean giving up the night. A decent pair of earplugs, a bit of distance from the speakers and a few breaks cut the risk a lot, and the music still sounds great.

**The short version:** loud events often hit around 100 decibels or more. At 100 dB, the US recommended workplace limit is used up in about 15 minutes. Wear earplugs for the whole night, stay away from the speaker stacks, and take quiet breaks. If your ears ring, that's a warning sign, not a badge of honor.

## How loud is a rave or a concert?

Sound is measured in decibels (dB). The scale is logarithmic, so every few decibels makes a big difference to the energy hitting your ears.

According to the US National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders (NIDCD), part of the NIH, here's roughly how loud everyday sounds get:

| Sound | Typical level |
|---|---|
| Whisper | 30 dBA |
| Normal conversation | 60 to 70 dBA |
| Movie theater | 74 to 104 dBA |
| Motorcycle | 80 to 110 dBA |
| Concerts, sporting events and headphones at full volume | 94 to 110 dBA |
| Ambulance siren | 110 to 129 dBA |
| Fireworks show | 140 to 160 dBA |

NIDCD says sounds at or below 70 dBA are generally considered safe. A dance floor sits well above that, and standing close to the speakers pushes it higher still.

The World Health Organization's global standard for safe listening venues and events recommends that venues keep the average level at or below **100 dB over any 15 minutes**. That's the ceiling the WHO considers acceptable, not a comfortable level, and plenty of nights go past it.

## How long can your ears take it?

The most useful way to think about loud sound is as a daily dose. The louder it is, the faster you use up your safe amount.

NIOSH, the US agency for workplace safety, sets its limit at 85 dBA averaged over an 8-hour day. It uses a 3 dB "exchange rate": every time the level goes up by 3 dB, the safe time is cut in half. That's how it gets to just 15 minutes at 100 dBA.

| Level | Time to reach a full daily dose (NIOSH) |
|---|---|
| 85 dBA | 8 hours |
| 88 dBA | 4 hours |
| 91 dBA | 2 hours |
| 94 dBA | 1 hour |
| 97 dBA | 30 minutes |
| 100 dBA | 15 minutes |
| 103 dBA | About 7.5 minutes |
| 106 dBA | Under 4 minutes |

One honest caveat: NIOSH wrote this limit for workplaces, and it says the limit isn't a recommendation for noise outside work. Still, it shows how quickly the clock runs. Five hours at 100 dB is about 20 times NIOSH's full daily limit.

## Does one loud night really cause damage?

It can. A 2016 randomized trial published in *JAMA Otolaryngology–Head & Neck Surgery* tested this at a real festival in Amsterdam. Fifty-one adults spent about four and a half hours at an outdoor festival where the average level was 100 dBA. Half were given earplugs and half weren't.

- **Temporary hearing loss** afterward showed up in **8%** of ears in the earplug group, compared with **42%** in the group without earplugs.
- **Ringing in the ears (tinnitus)** affected **12%** of the earplug group and **40%** of the unprotected group.

The researchers concluded that earplugs are effective at preventing temporary hearing loss after loud music.

"Temporary" is doing a lot of work there. In a 2009 study in mice, Sharon Kujawa and Charles Liberman found that noise causing only a temporary drop in hearing still destroyed up to about half of the connections between the inner ear's hair cells and the hearing nerve, even after the hearing tests came back normal. Researchers call this cochlear synaptopathy, or "hidden hearing loss." Whether the same thing happens the same way in people is still being studied. But it's a good reason not to treat ringing ears as harmless just because they settle down by Monday.

## What do ringing ears after a night out mean?

Ringing, buzzing or muffled hearing after a loud night means your ears took more than they could handle. A Cleveland Clinic article on the subject calls ringing after a concert "a sign you've already damaged your hearing."

If ringing or muffled hearing doesn't fade after a day or two, see a doctor or an audiologist.

**Sudden hearing loss is different, and urgent.** If you lose hearing quickly, often in one ear, NIDCD says to treat it as a medical emergency and see a doctor immediately. Treatment works best when it starts early, and NIDCD notes that waiting more than two to four weeks makes it less likely to help.

## Which earplugs are best for raves and concerts?

Any earplug is better than none. But the type you choose changes how the music sounds, and that decides whether you'll actually wear them.

### Foam earplugs

Cheap, easy to find and good at blocking sound. The downside is that they block high frequencies more than low ones, so music sounds muffled and voices get hard to follow. Earplug maker Etymotic Research makes the same point in its fitting guide: conventional earplugs cut highs more than lows and mids.

They're a great backup to keep in your bag. To get a proper seal, NIOSH recommends its "roll-pull-hold" method:

1. **Roll** the earplug into a thin snake between your fingers.
2. **Pull** the top of your ear up and back with your other hand to straighten the ear canal, then slide the plug in.
3. **Hold** it with a finger while it expands. Your own voice should sound muffled once it's sealed.

To check the fit, cup your hands over your ears. If that makes things much quieter, the plug isn't sealing properly, so take it out and try again.

### High-fidelity (musician's) earplugs

These are the ones most regulars end up using. They have a small filter that lowers the volume more evenly across frequencies, so the music sounds like a quieter version of itself instead of a muffled one. Etymotic's guide, for example, describes filters that cut roughly 9, 15 or 25 dB fairly evenly.

They're reusable, they come in a little case, and many are close to invisible once they're in. Keep a pair on your keyring so they come out with you every time.

### Custom-molded earplugs

An audiologist takes impressions of your ears and has plugs made to fit, usually with interchangeable filters. They cost more, but they're the most comfortable for long nights and they seal reliably. If you're out most weekends or you DJ, they're worth looking into.

### A note on NRR ratings

US earplug packaging shows a Noise Reduction Rating (NRR). It's measured in a lab, and real-world protection is usually lower. NIOSH's guidance is to knock the labelled number down: subtract 50% for foam-style earplugs and 70% for other earplugs. So treat the number on the box as a best case, and focus on a good fit.

## Other ways to protect your hearing at a show

Earplugs do most of the work, but these help too:

- **Don't stand next to the speakers.** Sound is most intense right in front of the stacks. Moving back, or off to the side, makes a real difference and you'll still hear everything.
- **Take quiet breaks.** Step outside, head to the bar area or find a chill-out space every hour or so. The WHO's venue standard includes quiet zones for exactly this reason.
- **Keep the plugs in all night.** Once your ears have had a lot, the room can start to sound quieter than it really is. Taking your earplugs out for "just this track" adds up.
- **Measure it.** NIOSH makes a free Sound Level Meter app for iPhone. In NIOSH's testing it came within about 2 dB of a professional reference meter. It isn't a replacement for professional equipment, but it's a quick reality check.
- **Watch the ride home.** Turning your headphones up to drown out the train after a loud night adds more dose to ears that have already had plenty.
- **Give your ears a rest the next day.** Keep the volume low and skip the gym's speaker-side spot.

## FAQ

### How many decibels is a club or rave?

It varies by venue and where you stand, but NIDCD puts concerts in the 94 to 110 dBA range. The WHO recommends venues keep the average at or below 100 dB over 15 minutes, which is a ceiling, not a safe listening level.

### Will earplugs ruin the music?

Foam earplugs can make music sound muffled. High-fidelity earplugs lower the volume more evenly across frequencies, so most people find the music still sounds clear, just quieter.

### Can you damage your hearing in one night?

Yes. In the 2016 Amsterdam festival trial, 42% of unprotected ears showed temporary hearing loss after about four and a half hours, compared with 8% of ears protected by earplugs.

### How long should my ears ring after a concert?

Ideally not at all, because ringing is a sign of damage. If ringing or muffled hearing hasn't faded after a day or two, get your hearing checked. If you suddenly lose hearing, treat it as an emergency and see a doctor right away.

### Are noise-cancelling headphones a good substitute?

Not for a club. They're designed to reduce background noise for your own audio, not to protect you on a dance floor, and you can't hear the set properly with them on. Earplugs made for music are the better tool.
