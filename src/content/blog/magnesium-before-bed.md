Magnesium has become the internet's favorite sleep supplement. It's in "sleepy girl mocktails", night-time powders and half the wellness videos on your feed. So does taking magnesium before bed actually help you sleep?

**The short answer:** maybe a little, for some people. The best trials show small improvements, mostly in people who already sleep badly, and the overall evidence is rated low quality. Magnesium isn't a sleeping pill, and it won't make up for a late night, a phone in bed or a 4 p.m. energy drink.

Here's what the research actually found.

## Why people link magnesium and sleep

Magnesium is an essential mineral that your body uses in hundreds of processes, including muscle and nerve function. Researchers have long noticed that people who get more magnesium tend to report better sleep. A 2022 systematic review in *Biological Trace Element Research* found that link in observational studies, but said the randomized trials showed an uncertain association between magnesium supplements and sleep.

That gap matters. Observational studies can't tell you whether magnesium improves sleep or whether people who eat well and sleep well simply have other things in common. Randomized trials, where some people get magnesium and some get a placebo, are how you find out. And those trials are where the story gets weaker.

## What the trials found

### Older adults with insomnia (2021 review)

A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis in *BMC Complementary Medicine and Therapies* found only three randomized trials comparing magnesium with a placebo, covering 151 older adults.

- People taking magnesium fell asleep **about 17 minutes faster** than people on placebo.
- Total sleep time went up by about 16 minutes, but that wasn't statistically significant.
- All three trials were at moderate to high risk of bias, and the evidence was rated low to very low quality.

The authors concluded the research wasn't good enough for doctors to make well-informed recommendations about magnesium for older adults with insomnia.

### Adults who sleep badly (2025 trial)

The biggest recent trial was published in 2025 in *Nature and Science of Sleep* by researchers at Leibniz University Hannover and Murdoch University. They gave 155 adults aged 18 to 65 who reported poor sleep either **250 mg of magnesium a day (as magnesium bisglycinate)** or a placebo.

- After 4 weeks, insomnia scores dropped by 3.9 points in the magnesium group and 2.3 points in the placebo group.
- That difference was just statistically significant (p = 0.049).
- The authors described the improvement as modest, and said future studies need objective sleep measurements and longer follow-up.

Notice how much the placebo group improved too. A lot of what people feel from a new sleep routine comes from the routine itself.

### The bigger picture (2026 review)

A 2026 systematic review of 12 placebo-controlled trials, reported by NutraIngredients, concluded that the evidence is inconsistent and of low certainty. Magnesium may give modest benefits to some groups, but the review found it doesn't support magnesium as a routine treatment for insomnia.

## Who might notice a difference?

In the 2025 trial, an exploratory analysis suggested people with **lower magnesium intake from food** might benefit more. That makes sense: topping up something you're short on is more likely to do something than adding more of what you already have.

You might be more likely to fall short if you:

- Eat few leafy greens, nuts, seeds, beans or whole grains
- Rely on takeout and ultra-processed food on busy weeks
- Drink heavily on a regular basis. The NIH lists alcohol use disorder among the things that lower magnesium levels.

If you already eat a varied diet and still sleep badly, the cause is more likely to be timing, light, caffeine, stress or a sleep disorder.

## Which form of magnesium is best for sleep?

There's no head-to-head trial, so nobody can honestly say one form is best for sleep.

What we know from the 2026 review:

- Only **one** of the 12 trials used magnesium bisglycinate (glycinate), even though it's the form most marketed for sleep.
- Other trials used magnesium L-threonate (3 trials), oxide (2), chloride (2), gluconate (1) and citrate (1).
- Some positive results came from oxide, bisglycinate, threonate and chloride. Trials using citrate didn't show consistent sleep benefits.

The form can affect how your stomach handles it. Magnesium citrate, for example, is also sold in much larger doses as a laxative. Our [guide to magnesium glycinate vs citrate](/blog/magnesium-glycinate-vs-citrate) explains the differences.

## How much magnesium is too much?

The NIH's numbers for adults:

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Recommended daily amount (from all sources) | 400 to 420 mg for men, 310 to 320 mg for women |
| Upper limit from **supplements and medicines** | 350 mg a day (food doesn't count) |

The 2025 trial used 250 mg a day, under that limit. Too much magnesium from supplements most often causes diarrhea, nausea and stomach cramps.

Magnesium also turns up in antacids, some laxatives and many "sleep" and "recovery" blends. Add them all up before you take more.

## When should you take magnesium?

No trial has compared taking magnesium at night with taking it in the morning. The trials gave it daily, and benefits (where they appeared) showed up over weeks, not on the first night.

So the best time is whenever you'll take it consistently. Take it with food if supplements upset your stomach.

## Who should check with a doctor first

- **People with kidney problems.** Your kidneys clear extra magnesium, so the risk of getting too much rises when they don't work well.
- **People taking certain medicines.** Magnesium can reduce how well some antibiotics (tetracyclines and quinolones like ciprofloxacin) and bone medicines (bisphosphonates) are absorbed. The NIH advises spacing them out. Our article on [taking zinc and magnesium together](/blog/can-you-take-zinc-and-magnesium-together) covers the timing.
- **People on long-term acid-reflux medicines or diuretics**, which can change your magnesium levels.

If you take any prescription medicine, a pharmacist can check your list in a couple of minutes.

## Sleep basics beat any supplement

Even the most positive magnesium trial found a small effect. These habits do far more:

- **Keep your wake-up time steady**, even after a late night. Our guide to [resetting your sleep after a weekend out](/blog/reset-sleep-after-a-weekend-out) walks through it.
- **Cut off caffeine early.** In one study, 400 mg of caffeine taken six hours before bed cut total sleep by more than an hour, and people didn't notice.
- **Don't use alcohol as a sleep aid.** It helps you drop off, then breaks up your sleep in the second half of the night.
- **Aim for 7 or more hours.** That's what the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends for adults.

## FAQ

### Does magnesium help you fall asleep faster?

In a 2021 review of three small trials in older adults, people taking magnesium fell asleep about 17 minutes faster than people on placebo. The evidence was rated low to very low quality, so treat it as a possible small benefit, not a promise.

### How long does magnesium take to work for sleep?

The trials ran for weeks, not nights. The 2025 trial measured its results after 4 weeks of daily use.

### Is magnesium glycinate the best magnesium for sleep?

It's the most popular, but only one trial has tested it for sleep, and it found a modest benefit. No study has compared it directly with other forms.

### Can I take magnesium every night?

Most healthy adults can, as long as the total from supplements and medicines stays at or under 350 mg a day. Check with a doctor first if you have kidney problems or take medicines that interact with it.

### Can you take too much magnesium?

Yes. More than 350 mg a day from supplements often causes diarrhea and stomach cramps. Very high doses can be dangerous, especially for people with kidney problems.
