A festival day is basically an endurance event with a better soundtrack. You're on your feet for hours, often in the sun, dancing in a crowd that traps heat, and you sweat far more than you notice. Staying hydrated is one of the simplest things you can do to feel better all weekend.

There's a catch most guides skip: drinking *too much* water is also dangerous. The goal isn't to drink as much as possible. It's to drink steadily, eat properly and know the warning signs.

**The short version:** sip regularly instead of chugging, let thirst guide you, and never drink more than about 1.4 liters (48 oz) an hour. Add a sports drink or salty food if you're sweating for hours. Eat real meals. If someone gets confused, stops sweating normally or collapses in the heat, get the medical team right away.

## How much water do you actually need?

The US National Academies set the baseline. Adequately hydrated adults take in on average about:

- **3.7 liters (125 oz) of total water a day for men**
- **2.7 liters (91 oz) a day for women**

That's *total* water, including food. About 80% comes from drinks and the other 20% from what you eat. The same report says most healthy people meet their needs by letting thirst be their guide, and that people who are very active or in hot climates may need more.

A festival day ticks both boxes. You're active for most of the day and often outside in the heat, so plan to drink more than you would on a normal day, spread out over the whole day.

## How much water should you drink at a festival?

There's no single number, because it depends on the heat, how hard you're dancing and how much you sweat. Two expert sources give useful guardrails.

**For hot conditions, NIOSH** (the US workplace safety agency) advises people working in the heat to drink 1 cup (8 oz, about 240 mL) of water every 15 to 20 minutes. That works out to about 24 to 32 oz (roughly 0.7 to 0.95 liters) an hour. It also says to drink before you feel thirsty.

**For long exercise, sports medicine experts** take a slightly different view. An international consensus panel on exercise-associated hyponatremia, published in the *Clinical Journal of Sport Medicine* in 2015, recommends drinking when you're thirsty, because forcing fluids is a main cause of dangerously low blood sodium.

The common ground is clear:

- **Sip steadily** through the day rather than downing a whole bottle at once.
- **Use thirst as your main signal**, and drink a bit more on very hot days when you're sweating hard.
- **Never go over about 48 oz (1.4 liters) an hour.** NIOSH sets that as the maximum, because drinking more can cause a medical emergency.

| Situation | What to aim for |
|---|---|
| Normal day | Drink when thirsty, plus water with meals |
| Hot day, dancing for hours | Regular sips, roughly a cup every 15 to 20 minutes when you're sweating hard |
| Sweating for several hours | Mix in a sports drink or salty food, not just water |
| Any situation | No more than about 1.4 liters (48 oz) an hour |

## Can you drink too much water?

Yes, and it's one of the most important things to know before a festival.

When you drink far more than you lose, the extra fluid dilutes the sodium in your blood. Doctors call it **hyponatremia**. The 2015 consensus statement says it can happen during or up to 24 hours after physical activity. Symptoms include headache, vomiting and confusion, and it can progress to seizures. Severe cases can be fatal without immediate treatment.

Two things make it tricky:

- **The symptoms overlap with heat exhaustion.** Headache and nausea show up in both, so it's easy to assume someone just needs more water.
- **Sports drinks don't prevent it.** The consensus panel says overdrinking any fluid, including sports drinks, is the main risk.

So if a friend has been drinking a lot of water and becomes confused, has a bad headache or starts vomiting, don't keep handing them bottles. Take them to the medical tent.

## Water or electrolytes?

For a few hours of normal activity, water is enough, as long as you're eating.

Electrolytes start to matter when you're sweating for a long time. NIOSH says that for prolonged sweating lasting several hours, sports drinks with balanced electrolytes are another option. It also warns that heavy use adds a lot of sugar.

The American College of Sports Medicine's position stand on fluid replacement explains why there's no one-size answer: sweat rates and the amount of sodium in sweat vary a lot from person to person. It also notes that drinks with electrolytes and carbohydrates can help more than water alone during long exercise.

In practice, for a long festival day:

- **Eat real meals.** Food supplies about a fifth of your water, and it contains salt that helps replace what you sweat out. Running on snacks all day makes everything harder.
- **Add electrolytes when you're sweating hard for hours.** A sports drink or an electrolyte mix in your bottle every so often is plenty. You don't need one in every bottle.
- **Remember that electrolytes don't make overdrinking safe.** The hourly limit still applies.

## Alcohol, caffeine and energy drinks

NIOSH's heat guidance includes two points that apply to a festival:

- **Alcohol** within 24 hours of being in the heat can increase the risk of heat illness.
- **Several energy drinks a day** can raise your caffeine levels enough to affect your heart.

If you're drinking, alternate with water and eat. If you're relying on energy drinks to keep going, that's a sign to sit down, cool off and have something to eat instead.

## Signs you're getting dehydrated

MedlinePlus, the NIH's health encyclopedia, lists these signs of **mild to moderate dehydration**:

- Thirst
- Dry or sticky mouth
- Not peeing much, or darker yellow pee
- Dry, cool skin
- Headache
- Muscle cramps

Signs of **severe dehydration** include very dark or no pee, confusion, dizziness, a rapid heartbeat, rapid breathing and listlessness. Severe dehydration needs medical help, not just a bottle of water.

## Heat exhaustion vs heat stroke

Festivals in summer add heat on top of everything else. The CDC's warning signs, published by the National Weather Service, are worth knowing:

| | Heat exhaustion | Heat stroke |
|---|---|---|
| **Signs** | Heavy sweating, weakness or tiredness, cool, pale, clammy skin, fast weak pulse, muscle cramps, dizziness, nausea or vomiting, headache, fainting | Throbbing headache, confusion, slurred speech, nausea, dizziness, body temperature above 103°F, hot, red, dry or damp skin, rapid strong pulse, fainting or loss of consciousness |
| **What to do** | Move somewhere cooler, loosen clothing, apply cool wet cloths and offer sips of water. Get medical help if they vomit, or if symptoms get worse or last more than an hour | **Call 911 or get the medical team immediately.** Move them somewhere cooler and cool them with wet cloths. **Do not give fluids** |

Heat stroke is a medical emergency. At a festival, the fastest route is usually the medical tent or any security or staff member, who can radio for help. Stay with the person until help arrives.

## A simple festival-day hydration plan

**Before you go**

- Drink normally with breakfast and lunch. You don't need to "preload" with huge amounts.
- Check the event's rules on bottles and hydration packs. Many allow an empty refillable bottle, but not all do.
- Find the free water and refill points on the map, along with the medical tent.

**During the day**

- Sip every time you pass a refill point or take a break.
- Eat a proper meal, not just snacks.
- Take shade breaks, especially in the middle of the afternoon.
- Add a sports drink or electrolytes if you've been sweating hard for hours.
- Check in with your friends. It's easier to spot confusion or someone getting too hot from the outside.

**At night**

- Keep sipping, but don't use the end of the night to make up for the whole day at once.
- Put a glass of water by your bed before you head out. Our [rave checklist](/blog/what-to-bring-to-a-rave) has more of these set-up-before-you-leave tips.

**The next day**

- Drink with breakfast, eat something salty and get outside into daylight. Here's [how to reset your sleep after a weekend out](/blog/reset-sleep-after-a-weekend-out).

## FAQ

### How much water should I drink at a festival?

Enough to stay ahead of thirst, spread out through the day. In heat, NIOSH's guide for people working outdoors is about a cup every 15 to 20 minutes. Never drink more than about 1.4 liters (48 oz) in an hour.

### Is it possible to drink too much water at a festival?

Yes. Drinking far more than you lose can dilute your blood sodium, a condition called hyponatremia. Symptoms include headache, vomiting and confusion, and severe cases are a medical emergency.

### Are electrolyte drinks better than water?

For long, sweaty days they can help, especially alongside meals. For shorter periods, water and food are enough. Electrolyte drinks don't make it safe to drink unlimited amounts.

### How do I know if I'm dehydrated?

Common early signs are thirst, a dry mouth, peeing less, darker yellow pee, headache and muscle cramps. Confusion, dizziness or a racing heart are signs of something more serious, so get medical help.

### What should I do if a friend collapses in the heat?

Get the medical team, security or staff immediately, or call 911. Move them somewhere cooler and cool them with wet cloths. If heat stroke is possible, don't give them fluids, and stay with them until help arrives.
